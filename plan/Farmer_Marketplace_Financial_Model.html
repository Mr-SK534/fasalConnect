How is this possible — where did the "extra" money come from?

This is the key insight to explain clearly, because at first glance it can look like the numbers don't add up:

  • In the traditional chain, ₹26/kg (65% of the ₹40 consumer price) is consumed by multiple intermediary layers — trader, mandi agent, wholesaler, retailer — each taking a margin.

  • In our model, only ₹4.60/kg (₹1.60 commission + ₹3 logistics = 11.5% of consumer price) goes to the platform layer.

  • The difference — ₹21.40/kg — is the inefficiency we remove. This is split between the farmer earning more (+₹6/kg vs. traditional) and the consumer paying less (−₹15.40/kg vs. traditional), while the platform still earns a sustainable margin on a much smaller cut.

In one line: everyone wins because we're not adding a new markup — we're replacing five inefficient markups with one efficient, transparent one.

6. Caveats to Mention Proactively (judge Q&A readiness)

  • This model assumes 100% sell-through at the farmer's ₹20/kg asking price — real-world demand may require dynamic pricing (this is exactly where the Prophet-based demand forecasting module adds value, by helping farmers set realistic, demand-aligned prices rather than a fixed guess).

  • The 35% traditional farmer-share figure is an industry-representative estimate for perishables, not a hard verified statistic for tomatoes specifically — worth citing a proper source (e.g. an ICRIER, NABARD, or Ministry of Agriculture value-chain study) if you want to defend this number rigorously in front of technical judges.

  • Platform commission (8%) and logistics fee (₹3/kg) are illustrative — final rates would need validation against real logistics partner quotes and competitor commission benchmarks (Ninjacart, DeHaat typically charge in a comparable range).

  • This is a single-season, single-crop snapshot — a full business case would model this across multiple crops, seasons, and farmer cohorts to show recurring revenue and scaling economics.
